Korea's largest listed companies pulled in opposite directions this week: Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) closed more than 6% higher on August 26 after reports that it may take an equity stake in the American nuclear firm Westinghouse, while Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix gave back an early-week advance, falling into the 3–4% range on August 28 as investors turned cautious ahead of the Federal Reserve's Jackson Hole address.

## A Nuclear Catalyst for the State Utility

KEPCO's jump was the sharpest single-name move of the week among Korean large caps. The state-controlled utility finished the August 26 regular session up more than 6% after reports circulated that it could invest in Westinghouse, the U.S. reactor builder. The reports remain unconfirmed, and no formal disclosure of an investment has been made — the move reflects investor positioning around the possibility rather than a completed transaction.

The speculation carries weight because of how KEPCO's corporate footprint is built. Its subsidiary KEPCO E&C specializes in designing and engineering nuclear power facilities, making the group a direct participant in the reactor-export business where Westinghouse has been both a rival and, at times, a contractual counterparty. Any equity relationship between the two would reshape the competitive terrain for overseas nuclear projects, which explains why the market repriced the stock so quickly on reports alone.

## Chipmakers Give Back a Two-Day Advance

The country's two memory-chip heavyweights traced a full round trip within three sessions. On August 26, Samsung Electronics opened flat and closed 1.8% higher, with SK Hynix adding 0.6%. By August 28, the tone had reversed: both stocks opened around 1% lower, and the weakness deepened through the day, with Samsung Electronics ending down in the 3% range and SK Hynix falling more than 4%.

Two forces appear to have converged. First, the lift that had tracked Nvidia's momentum — a persistent driver for Korean memory names given their role supplying the AI hardware chain — showed signs of running out. Second, traders reduced exposure ahead of the Fed chair's speech at the Jackson Hole symposium, an event that routinely resets expectations for U.S. interest-rate policy and, by extension, appetite for Asian tech equities.

The stakes for the Korean market are structural rather than episodic. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, together with Micron, form the trio that dominates global memory-chip production, and SK Hynix alone reported roughly 97.15 trillion won in revenue and about 42.95 trillion won in net profit for fiscal 2025. When both names swing several percentage points in a session, the index-level effect in Seoul is difficult for any other sector to offset.

## An Ownership Filing in the Background

Separately, a large equity-holdings report concerning Samsung Electronics shares was filed with the Financial Supervisory Service's DART disclosure system, submitted by Samsung C&T. The filing itself is a routine regulatory instrument for reporting significant shareholdings, but the filer matters: Samsung C&T sits at the center of the group's ownership structure, tracing its lineage to the conglomerate's founding trading business established in 1938. Investors watching governance dynamics at Korea's largest company treat movements in that stake as a standing item, even when the disclosure is procedural.

## Where the Balance of Risk Sits Now

The week's pattern underlines how differently the two stories are priced. KEPCO's gain rests on an unconfirmed strategic possibility, which means the stock carries headline risk in both directions until the company or Westinghouse's owners clarify their intentions. The chipmakers' pullback, by contrast, is tied to a scheduled macro event and an earnings-driven sentiment cycle around AI hardware — factors that will resolve on a known calendar. For Seoul-listed equities as a group, the next directional cue is less likely to come from Korea than from what the Federal Reserve chooses to say.

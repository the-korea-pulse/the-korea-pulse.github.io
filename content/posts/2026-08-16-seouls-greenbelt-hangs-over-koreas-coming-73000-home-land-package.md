South Korea's government intends to designate new public housing districts totaling at least 73,000 homes, with an announcement possible as early as next month — but the question that will define the package is still open: whether any of Seoul's greenbelt, the ring of protected green space that has constrained the capital's outward growth for decades, will be released for development. Seoul Mayor Oh Se-hoon has publicly opposed sacrificing green space, while Land Minister Kim Yun-deok intends to search for common ground with the city before the plan is finalized.

## Why Everything Turns on Seoul

The headline number — 73,000 homes "plus alpha," leaving room for additions — matters less than where those homes end up. Housing demand in Korea is overwhelmingly concentrated in Seoul and its immediate commuting belt, and large tracts of undeveloped land inside the city limits exist almost exclusively within the greenbelt. A supply package that draws mostly on sites in outlying Gyeonggi Province would carry far less weight for Seoul prices than one that unlocks land inside the capital itself. That is why the greenbelt question, rather than the unit count, is the axis of the coming announcement.

## A Trading Freeze Before the Reveal

Ahead of the designation, green-space areas across 19 of Seoul's autonomous districts have been placed under temporary land-transaction permit restrictions, which require government approval for purchases and are designed to shut speculators out of areas that may be tapped for development. Such freezes are a standard precursor to new-town designations in Korea precisely because leaks about candidate sites have historically triggered land-buying rushes that inflate compensation costs. The breadth of the restriction — covering green areas in most of the city's districts — keeps the actual candidate sites obscured while preserving the government's options.

## City Hall Against the Central Government

The unresolved conflict is institutional as much as environmental. Oh has staked out a position against damaging green space, framing the greenbelt as an asset that should not be traded away for supply targets. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, which drives national housing-supply policy, needs the city's cooperation to make any Seoul designation workable in practice — from local permitting to infrastructure planning. Kim's stated intent to seek a compromise with the city suggests the final package could land anywhere on a spectrum from a symbolic, limited release of already-degraded greenbelt parcels to a plan that leaves Seoul's protected land untouched and shifts the burden outward.

## What the Timing Implies

An announcement window of late next month gives both governments limited room to close the gap. If no agreement is reached, the government retains legal authority over public housing district designation, but proceeding over the city's objection would invite the kind of drawn-out friction that has slowed past supply drives. The shape of the compromise — or the absence of one — will say as much about the balance of power between Seoul City Hall and the central government as it does about housing policy itself.

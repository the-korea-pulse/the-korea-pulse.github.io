Korea East-West Power (EWP) formally welcomed 121 new employees on August 14, holding its second-half 2026 induction ceremony in the main auditorium of its Ulsan headquarters and using the occasion to underline two priorities for its incoming class: workplace safety and digital-technology capability. The state-run generator, one of the utilities spun off from Korea Electric Power Corporation, also emphasized that the intake reflects an expanded commitment to socially equitable recruitment, including candidates from outside the Seoul capital region and applicants from families of national merit.

## A Class Shaped by Balanced-Hiring Policy

The composition of the new cohort is as notable as its size. EWP said it widened recruitment channels aimed at non-capital-region talent and veterans-affairs candidates, extending a broader push among Korean public institutions to spread employment opportunity beyond the Seoul metropolitan area. For a company headquartered in Ulsan — an industrial city whose economy leans heavily on shipbuilding, autos, and energy — regional hiring is more than a compliance exercise; it anchors a skilled workforce in the southeast at a time when local governments are working to slow the outflow of young workers to the capital.

## Safety and Digital Capability as the Core Curriculum

The company framed safety awareness and digital competency as the defining requirements for its newest employees. That pairing tracks the twin pressures facing Korea's thermal and renewable generators: tighter safety expectations across industrial sites, and an operational shift toward data-driven plant management that demands software fluency alongside traditional engineering skills. The ceremony itself gestured at that direction — the new hires produced a video using AI tools and screened it during the event, a small but telling signal of how the company expects its junior ranks to work.

## What a Mid-Year Intake Says About Public Energy Employers

Korea's public energy companies hire on a semiannual rhythm, and a triple-digit second-half class suggests EWP is maintaining steady workforce replenishment even as the generation sector navigates fuel-cost volatility and the long transition away from coal. Public institutions are often counter-cyclical employers in Korea, sustaining graduate hiring when private-sector recruitment tightens, and induction classes like this one function as a visible commitment to that role. For jobseekers, the emphasis on veterans' and regional tracks is a reminder that the entry routes into public energy companies are broader than the standard open competition — and that Ulsan, not Seoul, is where many of those careers will begin.

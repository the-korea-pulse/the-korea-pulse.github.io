South Korea and Japan held the fifth round of their bilateral economic security dialogue in Tokyo on September 1, where the two governments discussed ways to deepen cooperation on artificial intelligence, semiconductor development, and critical mineral supply chains, South Korea's presidential office said.

## A Recurring Channel, Now With an AI Agenda

The dialogue is not a one-off summit deliverable but an institutionalized channel — this was its fifth session — and the agenda has been drifting toward the technologies that now define economic statecraft. According to the presidential office, the Tokyo round covered joint approaches to fostering AI and semiconductor industries alongside the more established supply-chain items, with critical minerals featuring prominently.

That pairing is telling. Korea and Japan sit in structurally similar positions: both are advanced manufacturing economies with world-class positions in chips and batteries, and both are heavily dependent on imported raw materials for those same industries. Critical mineral processing is concentrated in a small number of countries, which leaves Seoul and Tokyo exposed to the same chokepoints. A bilateral channel that treats mineral sourcing and AI industrial policy as parts of one conversation reflects how both governments now define economic security — less about tariffs, more about who controls the inputs to next-generation technology.

## Why AI Is Now Framed as a Security Question

Placing AI development inside an economic security dialogue, rather than a trade or science forum, indicates both sides see the technology's foundations — advanced chips, compute infrastructure, and the materials beneath them — as strategic assets to be secured rather than simply traded. For Korea, whose economy leans heavily on semiconductor exports, and for Japan, which is investing to rebuild its chipmaking base, aligning AI industrial ambitions with supply-chain resilience is a natural, if still early-stage, convergence.

The presidential office's readout did not detail concrete agreements from the session, and the announced agenda — cooperation "방안," or ways forward — suggests the Tokyo round was about scoping options rather than signing deals. What can be said with confidence is that the channel survived the leadership transitions and diplomatic turbulence of recent years and is now meeting regularly at a working level.

## The Bilateral Track Within a Larger Realignment

The dialogue also fits a broader pattern of Seoul-Tokyo economic normalization that has accelerated since 2023, when export-control disputes between the two countries were wound down. Supply-chain coordination between the two U.S. allies has since expanded through both bilateral and trilateral formats, and economic security — a framing both governments have written into law and bureaucratic structure — has become the vocabulary through which that cooperation is organized.

Whether the fifth round produces follow-through will depend on what the two sides do between sessions: joint stockpiling or sourcing arrangements for minerals, coordinated positions on export controls, or concrete AI research and standards cooperation would be the markers to look for. For now, the substance is the continuity itself — two neighboring technology powers treating each other as partners in securing the industrial base, meeting on schedule, with an agenda that keeps growing.

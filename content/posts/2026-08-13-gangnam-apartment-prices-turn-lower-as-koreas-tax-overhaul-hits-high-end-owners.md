Apartment prices in Seocho and Gangnam, the two districts that anchor Seoul's high-end housing market, turned negative in the latest weekly reading, even as price growth across the capital as a whole merely slowed. The immediate trigger is the government's 2026 tax reform plan, which raises the tax burden on owners of expensive homes — and the neighborhoods with the most expensive homes are reacting first.

## Why the Turn Started South of the River

Gangnam and Seocho function as the reference point for Korean housing. The districts concentrate the country's priciest apartment complexes, and their weekly price moves are watched as a leading indicator for the wider Seoul market. That status cuts both ways: when policy raises the cost of holding an expensive home, these districts absorb the shock before anywhere else.

That is the pattern now visible. Citywide, Seoul apartment prices are still rising, but at a reduced pace. In Seocho and Gangnam, the weekly figures have crossed from gains into declines — a reversal rather than a mere deceleration, and a split between the top of the market and the rest of it.

## A Tax Plan Aimed at the Top of the Market

The finance ministry's 2026 tax reform plan increases the tax load carried by owners of high-priced housing. For households whose wealth is concentrated in a single expensive apartment — a common structure in Gangnam-area ownership — a heavier recurring tax bill changes the arithmetic of holding on. Some owners bring listings forward; prospective buyers, seeing the same arithmetic, wait. Price momentum fades from both sides of the trade at once.

Two caveats belong next to that mechanism. First, the reform is at this stage a government plan: the package must still pass the National Assembly, and tax bills are routinely amended on the way through. Second, announcement effects tend to be sharpest immediately after release, when owners and buyers are repricing the rules rather than responding to enacted law.

## The Difference Between a Dip and a Turn

One weekly decline does not establish a trend. Weekly price indices move in small increments, and a single negative print in two districts can be unwound as quickly as it appeared — particularly if the final legislation lands softer than the announced plan.

The more telling test is breadth. If the declines stay confined to Seocho and Gangnam while the rest of Seoul keeps rising slowly, this reads as a targeted repricing of the specific homes the tax plan touches. If other expensive areas follow the same path in the coming weeks, the announcement will have marked the point where Seoul's long price climb began to bend. In earlier tightening episodes, the southern districts have tended to lead the rest of the capital in both directions — which is precisely why this week's reversal, however small, is the number the market is watching.

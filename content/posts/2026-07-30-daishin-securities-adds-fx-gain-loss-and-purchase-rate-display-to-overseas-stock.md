Daishin Securities [003540] has redesigned its overseas stock balance screen so that investors can now see their foreign-exchange gains and losses alongside the exchange rate at which they originally bought each holding, the Seoul-based brokerage announced on July 30. The change targets a long-standing blind spot for Korean retail investors in U.S. and other foreign equities: how much of their return comes from the stock itself, and how much from the won's movement against the currency it trades in.

## Splitting the Stock Return From the Currency Return

For a Korean investor holding dollar-denominated shares, total return is always a composite. A U.S. stock can rise while a strengthening won erases the gain in Korean-currency terms, or a flat stock can show a profit purely because the won weakened after purchase. Until now, most balance screens presented a single blended valuation, leaving investors to reconstruct the currency component on their own.

The revamped screen breaks that composite apart. By displaying the purchase exchange rate — the won-per-dollar level locked in when the position was funded — next to a running tally of currency gains and losses, the interface lets an account holder read both legs of the trade at a glance. The company framed the overhaul as a convenience upgrade for its overseas-equity customers.

## A Feature Aimed at an FX-Sensitive Investor Base

The timing is not incidental. Korean retail participation in foreign stock markets has grown into a structural feature of the country's household portfolio, and with it, sensitivity to the won-dollar rate has become a daily concern rather than an afterthought. When exchange-rate swings are large relative to underlying stock moves, the currency line item can dominate a portfolio's short-term performance — precisely the information the old balance screens obscured.

Transparency of this kind also carries practical decision-making weight. An investor deciding whether to realize a gain, convert proceeds back to won, or leave funds parked in dollars needs to know their effective cost basis in currency terms, not just in share price. Surfacing the purchase rate makes that calculation immediate.

## Interface Design as a Competitive Lever

Among Korean brokerages, overseas-trading services have become a key battleground for retail customers, and much of that competition now plays out in app and screen design rather than headline commission rates. Features that reduce the cognitive work of managing a multi-currency portfolio are one way a mid-sized house like Daishin can differentiate its trading platform. Whether rivals follow with comparable FX-visibility tools will be a reasonable test of how much this kind of disclosure matters to the investors both sides are courting.

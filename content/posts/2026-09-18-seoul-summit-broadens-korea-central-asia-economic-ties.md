South Korea used its inaugural summit with Central Asian nations in Seoul to broaden economic engagement beyond diplomacy, pairing government agreements with private-sector outreach in critical minerals, health regulation and infrastructure. Uzbekistan emerged as a key partner, while Daewoo Engineering & Construction’s participation underscored Korean companies’ interest in future projects across the region.

## Uzbekistan Deals Target Strategic Industries

The Korean government announced a memorandum of understanding with Uzbekistan on critical-mineral supply chains. The agreement reflects Seoul’s effort to secure more resilient access to resources needed for advanced manufacturing while developing economic links with mineral-producing countries.

A separate memorandum between the two countries covers regulatory cooperation for medical products. That arrangement could help Korean and Uzbek authorities exchange regulatory knowledge and create a clearer foundation for pharmaceutical and medical-device cooperation.

Together, the agreements show that the relationship is moving into specialized areas where government coordination can lower barriers for subsequent commercial activity.

## Daewoo E&C Positions for Regional Projects

Daewoo E&C Chairman Jung Won-ju joined the summit’s official program and pursued expanded cooperation in Central Asia, according to the company. His involvement points to potential demand for Korean expertise in urban development, transport, energy facilities and other large-scale construction.

The Seoul-headquartered builder began operations in 1973 and has a long record of overseas engineering and construction. Its presence at the summit indicates that Korean contractors view closer state-level relations as a possible opening for project development, although no new construction contract was disclosed.

## Agreements Still Need Commercial Follow-Through

The summit created a broad institutional framework, but memoranda do not by themselves guarantee investment or procurement. Critical-mineral cooperation will depend on commercially viable projects, processing arrangements and reliable transport routes. Medical-product cooperation will require regulators to translate their memorandum into practical exchanges and compatible procedures.

For construction companies, the key test will be whether diplomatic engagement produces bankable projects with clear financing, tender rules and risk allocation. The Seoul meetings widened the field for cooperation; the next phase will determine which commitments become operating partnerships.

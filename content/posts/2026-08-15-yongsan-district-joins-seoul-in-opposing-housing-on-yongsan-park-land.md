South Korea's drive to expand housing supply has collided with one of Seoul's most symbolically charged pieces of land. After the transport and land minister said the government would review the entire Yongsan Park area as a candidate site for residential development, Yongsan District — the borough that hosts the park — declared its strong opposition, following the Seoul Metropolitan Government in rejecting the idea outright. The district's core argument: Yongsan Park is a space of national significance, not a land bank for apartment blocks.

## Two Levels of Government, One Refusal

The review was floated by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport, the cabinet-rank body headquartered in Sejong City that steers the country's housing policy. Its minister, Kim Yoon-deok, who has held the post since July 19, 2025 under President Lee Jae-myung, indicated that the whole of the Yongsan Park area was on the table for housing supply.

Seoul's city government objected first. Yongsan District then added its own statement of strong opposition, framing the park as a "nationally symbolic space" that should not be diverted to residential construction. That two successive layers of local government — the metropolitan city and the district itself — have now publicly pushed back leaves the ministry's trial balloon conspicuously without a local sponsor.

The blunt framing in Korean commentary — a "supply first, questions later" posture attributed to the government — captures why the episode resonates beyond one park. The administration has made expanding housing supply in Seoul a priority, and large, centrally located, state-controlled land is scarce. Yongsan is the rare parcel that is both.

## A Century of Claims on the Same Ground

The land's history explains why "symbolic" is not rhetorical inflation here. From 1906 until Korea's liberation in 1945, the grounds served as a military installation for colonial Japan. After the Korean War, the site became Yongsan Garrison, headquarters of the United States military in South Korea, complete with a base golf course.

The effort to return the land to civilian use has been running for nearly four decades: a presidential decree issued in December 1988 launched the conversion process. One early product of that effort, Yongsan Family Park, opened to the public in November 1992 on roughly 75,900 square meters of the former garrison grounds, with a walking trail of about two kilometers and Taegeukgi Park at its center. The far larger national park envisioned for the returned garrison land has been positioned as the culmination of that recovery — which is precisely the narrative that housing development on the site would interrupt.

For opponents, the arithmetic of symbolism is simple: land that took a century to reclaim from foreign military use, and almost forty years of deliberate policy to convert into public space, should not be reallocated in a single housing package.

## Where the Standoff Leaves Housing Policy

The immediate question is whether the ministry treats the review as a firm plan or an opening position. Local government opposition does not by itself veto central government land-use decisions, but a project of this scale and visibility is difficult to advance against the unified resistance of both the city and the district — particularly when the objection is not technical but about national identity.

The likelier near-term paths are a narrowing of scope — reviewing peripheral parcels rather than "the entire" park area — or a quiet retreat to other supply candidates in the capital region. Either way, the episode is a useful measure of the constraint facing Korean housing policy: the sites large enough to matter for supply are, almost by definition, the sites contested enough to stall. How the government resolves Yongsan will indicate how hard it is prepared to push that trade-off elsewhere in Seoul.

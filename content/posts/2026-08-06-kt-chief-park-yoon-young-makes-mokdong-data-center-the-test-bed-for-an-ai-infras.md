KT chief executive Park Yoon-young carried out an on-site inspection of the company's Mokdong data center in Seoul, using the visit to press for a group-wide mobilization behind KT's artificial intelligence data center business. Park's message, delivered at one of the carrier's flagship facilities, was that competitiveness in AIDC — the industry shorthand for data centers purpose-built for AI workloads — will depend on KT's affiliates pulling in the same direction rather than operating as separate silos.

## A Site Visit That Doubles as a Strategy Statement

Chief executives rarely tour server halls without a reason, and Park's choice of venue is itself the message. By walking the floor at Mokdong and pairing the inspection with a call to concentrate the group's capabilities, Park framed data center infrastructure as a corporate priority rather than a back-office utility. The remarks tie the facility directly to KT's ambitions in AI: the company is positioning its physical infrastructure — power, cooling, land, and network — as the foundation on which its AI business will stand or fall.

## The Carrier's Structural Advantage

KT, which operated under the name Korea Telecom before adopting its current identity, grew out of the business of building and running the country's communications backbone. That heritage matters here. A legacy carrier holds assets that AI-era entrants find hardest to replicate: existing data center sites in and around Seoul, dense fiber routes, and decades of experience operating facilities around the clock. Mokdong belongs to that inherited estate, and Park's inspection suggests the company intends to sweat those assets for the AI cycle rather than cede the ground to newer specialist operators.

## What "Pooling Group Capabilities" Would Take

Park's call to consolidate group capabilities is easier said than done. An AIDC business cuts across organizational lines — the network arm supplies connectivity, cloud and IT units bring customers and workloads, and the real-estate and facilities side controls sites and power contracts. Turning those pieces into a single competitive offering would require coordinated investment decisions and, most likely, a clearer internal division of labor than a conglomerate structure naturally produces. The inspection reads as an early move in that direction: a public signal from the top that the data center portfolio, starting with sites like Mokdong, is now a shared group project rather than any single unit's concern. How quickly that translates into concrete capacity plans or investment commitments remains to be seen.
